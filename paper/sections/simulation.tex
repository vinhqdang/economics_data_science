\section{Monte Carlo evidence}\label{sec:sim}

\subsection{Design}

The simulation examines the finite-sample behaviour of the estimator as it is used in the applications, with $G$ and $\kappa$ chosen on a hold-out; it does not verify the rates of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, which concern the exact minimiser for a fixed $G$.
It is deliberately simpler than the applications: units are independent, the data are stationary, and costs are linear, so it isolates the trade-off between the noise of unit-specific weights and the bias of pooled weights.

We simulate $N\in\{50,200\}$ units and $M=5$ candidate commitment rules.
Unit $i$ has demand $y_{it}=\mu_i+x_{it}$, with $\mu_i\sim U[20,100]$ and $x_{it}$ a stationary AR(1) process with coefficient 0.5 and standard deviation 5, and critical ratio $\tau_i\sim U[0.6,0.95]$ ($u_i=\tau_i/(1-\tau_i)$, $o_i=1$).
Candidate $m$ forecasts $f_{itm}=y_{it}-e_{itm}$, where the errors follow AR(1) processes with coefficient 0.5 and innovations with unit-specific scales $s_{im}$ and skewness signs $k_{im}\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ (a centred gamma distribution for $\pm1$, a normal distribution for 0).
Each candidate becomes a commitment by adding the $\tau_i$-quantile of its errors in a pre-sample of 200 periods.
Three designs define the heterogeneity:
\emph{grouped}---every unit belongs to one of three segments with segment-specific error profiles $(s_{i\cdot},k_{i\cdot})$, so that different candidates are best in different segments;
\emph{homogeneous}---all units share the first profile;
\emph{continuous}---each unit's error scales are a Dirichlet(1,1,1) mixture of the three profiles, so that no two units share their optimal weights and GDMA's segment structure is misspecified.
Weights are estimated on $T\in\{48,168,672\}$ periods and evaluated on 1,000 new periods.
We report the out-of-sample cost relative to an infeasible oracle that uses, for every unit, the exact cost-minimising weights on the evaluation sample itself.
Each cell uses 50 replications.

Table~\ref{tab:sim} supports the theory and clarifies when grouping pays.

\emph{Soft GDMA is the most reliable method.}
It has the lowest cost, or is within 0.1 percentage points of the lowest, in every one of the 18 cells.
Its advantage is largest when windows are short: with true segments and $T=48$ it is 3.5--4.1\% above the oracle against 8.4--8.6\% for unit-specific weights, and even under continuous heterogeneity, where segments are misspecified, it beats unit-specific weights (6.2--6.8\% against 9.1--9.4\% above the oracle) and shrinkage towards pooled weights (7.5\%).
When units share one error profile it matches pooled weights for short windows and beats them for long ones, where the heterogeneity in critical ratios makes some unit-level adjustment worthwhile.

\emph{Hard segments trade bias for variance exactly as Theorem~\ref{thm:oracle} predicts.}
Plain GDMA beats unit-specific weights in 96--100\% of the replications with true segments and $T=48$, and in all replications with homogeneous units for $T\le168$.
With long windows ($T=672$), unit-specific weights are estimated precisely and become slightly better (1.2--1.3\% against 1.7--1.8\% above the oracle).
Here GDMA selects five to six segments rather than three, because units within a true segment have different critical ratios and therefore different optimal weights: with enough data the hold-out rule correctly detects this finer structure, which is also why the ARI falls from about 0.9 at $T=48$ to about 0.65 at $T=672$.
Soft GDMA removes this residual bias through unit-level shrinkage and is never worse than unit-specific weights by more than 0.1 percentage points.

\emph{Joint estimation recovers segments better than clustering estimated weights.}
With short windows, the ARI of GDMA is 0.87--0.93, against 0.75--0.79 for two-step $k$-means on noisy unit-level weights, and GDMA's cost is lower in every grouped cell with $T\le168$.
When units share one error profile, the one-standard-error rule selects a single segment in most replications with $T\le168$ ($\bar G=1.0$--$1.3$).
