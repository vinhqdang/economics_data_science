\section{Managerial implications and conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Implications for system operators and planners.}
Five lessons emerge for organisations that coordinate many operating units, from grid operators and regulators to international bodies that fund energy-transition programmes.
First, the object to be learned is the \emph{decision rule}, not the forecast: the cost-minimising combination of forecasting systems depends on the cost asymmetry each unit faces, and combining for accuracy leaves 3--16\% of the achievable saving on the table, and in data-poor systems can even raise cost.
Second, planners need not choose between one rule for every system and a bespoke rule for each.
A handful of data-driven segments, with each unit shrunk towards its segment's rule, captures the heterogeneity that matters, is estimated with far less noise than bespoke rules, and produces a segmentation that can be discussed with operators---who is grouped with whom, and why.
Third, more flexible is not better: a neural mixture-of-experts gate with thousands of parameters, trained on the same decision loss, was nearly ten percentage points more expensive worldwide than soft GDMA, much more expensive for systems with little history, and made a third of the grid areas worse off.
Fourth, fairness need not cost efficiency: counting every area's relative improvement equally produced rules that were both cheaper in total and more evenly beneficial, and in storage siting a maximin rule protected the worst-served continent at a price of fairness of about 1\%, whereas the intuitive demand-proportional ``fair share'' made the worst-served continent worse off.
Fifth, forecasting and investment are linked.
Better commitment rules are worth several gigawatts of batteries, and a storage programme should be sited on the risk that remains after operations have been improved; siting it on today's error profile misplaces a meaningful share of a small programme's value.
Because GDMA only re-weights forecasts that operators already produce, it can be adopted without replacing existing forecasting systems.

\paragraph{Who would adopt it.}
The grouping needs someone who sees many units at once.
Natural adopters are organisations that already coordinate several operators---a regional transmission organisation computing rules for its member balancing authorities, the regional coordination centres of European transmission system operators, the market operator of a multi-region market such as AEMO---and forecasting-service providers or regulators that work with many utilities.
The data requirement is each unit's candidate commitments and realised demand over the estimation window, which such coordinators typically hold already; units that cannot share them could keep their data and exchange only the gradients of their own cost with respect to the shared weights, as in federated learning.
The rules are auditable because each unit's commitment remains a convex combination of commitments it already understands, and the segment memberships can be discussed with the operators concerned.
Operators remain responsible for reliability; GDMA is a way to set the day-ahead commitment that feeds their existing processes, not a replacement for reserve procurement or unit commitment.

\paragraph{Limitations and extensions.}
Our coverage of Africa and parts of Asia is thin, because public hourly demand data for South Africa, India, Japan and South Korea could not be retrieved in our computing environment; the method is designed for exactly such data-poor systems, the audit shows that they still benefit less than data-rich ones, and extending the panel is a priority. The fairness analysis measures income at the level of countries and U.S.\ states; the distribution of benefits across the households served within an area would require data we do not have.
The cost calibration is stylised: shortfall costs are tied to a transparent proxy of import flexibility rather than to market prices, and the single-period newsvendor abstracts from ramping, network constraints and multi-day commitments.
The weather candidates use archived GFS and GEFS forecasts at 0.5--1 degree resolution, which are public only from 2020--2021. With humidity, wind, radiation and the ensemble spread they halve the gap to the operators' forecasts during Winter Storm Elliott, but the combinations remain 3\% more expensive than the operators over those days even though one candidate on its own is 8\% cheaper: weights learned from the previous weeks cannot anticipate a storm. Letting the combination weights respond to the ensemble spread, and using higher-resolution or regional forecasts, are natural extensions.
The storage stage takes the commitment rule as given; jointly optimising commitments and battery dispatch, and adding the arbitrage and adequacy value of storage, would make the siting problem richer.
The theory treats the number of segments as given and relies on a high-level concentration condition; a formal analysis of the hold-out choice of $G$ and $\kappa$, and of strong cross-sectional dependence through common weather shocks, is left for future work.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Grouped decision-focused model averaging combines three ideas that have so far developed separately---optimal model averaging, decision-focused learning and latent group structures---into one estimator with finite-sample and asymptotic guarantees, and links it to the siting of new capacity.
On five continents it turns the forecasts operators already have into commitment rules that are cheaper, statistically stable and interpretable, and it tells planners where new storage is worth most once those rules are in place.
