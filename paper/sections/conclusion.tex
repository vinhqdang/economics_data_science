\section{Managerial implications and conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Implications for system operators and planners}
Five lessons emerge for organisations that coordinate many operating units.
First, learn the \emph{decision rule}, not the forecast: combining for accuracy and adding a margin leaves 2--3 points of the saving on the table in the United States and 8--10 points worldwide.
Second, planners need not choose between one rule for all systems and a bespoke rule for each: a handful of segments, or even one shared rule, with unit-level shrinkage captures most of the heterogeneity with far less noise, and the segmentation can be discussed with operators. The evidence for grouping itself is modest: it is statistically indistinguishable from simpler clustering rules.
Third, more flexible is not better: a neural mixture-of-experts gate with thousands of parameters was seven percentage points more expensive worldwide than soft GDMA and made 42\% of the grid areas worse off than their own best candidate.
Fourth, fairness in the sense of the criterion was nearly free, whereas maximin storage siting cost 7--9\% of the programme's value in price of fairness.
Fifth, forecasting and investment are linked: in a stylised model, the reference rule needs 34\,GW of optimally sited batteries to match the cost that soft GDMA achieves without any, and a programme sited on today's error profile loses 2--4\% of its value; this is an interaction to study, not a siting recommendation.
Because GDMA only re-weights existing forecasts, it can be adopted without replacing forecasting systems.

\paragraph{Who would adopt it}
The grouping needs someone who sees many units at once: a regional transmission organisation computing rules for its member balancing authorities, European regional coordination centres, a multi-region market operator such as AEMO, or forecasting-service providers and regulators that work with many utilities.
The data required are each unit's candidate commitments and realised demand, which such coordinators typically hold; units that cannot share them could exchange only cost gradients, as in federated learning.
The rules are auditable, as each commitment is a convex combination of commitments the unit already understands.
GDMA feeds the day-ahead commitment into existing processes and does not replace reserve procurement or unit commitment.

\paragraph{Limitations and extensions}
Coverage of Africa and parts of Asia is thin, because public hourly demand data for South Africa, India, Japan and South Korea could not be retrieved and the Chinese provinces are observed in a single year; such data-poor systems benefit much less. Income is measured for countries and U.S.\ states, not households.
Outside the United States the reference is a model forecast, because official day-ahead forecasts of European transmission system operators and of AEMO could not be retrieved, and in the United States the reference is a stylised policy, the official forecast plus a rolling quantile margin.
Shortfall costs rest on a proxy based on the variability of interchange, which is not transfer capability, rather than on market prices or the value of lost load, and the single-period newsvendor ignores ramping, network constraints and multi-day commitments, and demand served in a load-shedding event understates true demand.
The weather candidates use archived GFS and GEFS forecasts at 0.5--1 degree resolution, from late 2020 only and only where the 00~UTC run is available before gate closure; they roughly halve the gap to the operators' forecasts during Winter Storm Elliott, but the combinations remain about 10\% more expensive than the operators over those days, even though one candidate designed with that storm in view is 7\% cheaper on its own: weights learned from previous weeks cannot anticipate a storm.
The storage stage takes the commitment rule as given and values only commitment errors, so joint optimisation and other value streams could change where storage should go.
The theory treats the number of segments as given and relies on a high-level concentration condition; the hold-out choice of $G$ and $\kappa$ and strong cross-sectional dependence through common weather shocks are left for future work.

\paragraph{Conclusion}
Grouped decision-focused model averaging combines optimal model averaging, decision-focused learning and latent group structures in one estimator with guarantees for the idealised version, and links it to a stylised siting problem.
Using only information available at the day-ahead gate closure, it turns forecasts operators already have into commitment rules that are 12\% cheaper worldwide, and 13--17\% cheaper in the United States, than committing on a reference forecaster; the gain comes mostly from deciding for the decision and sharing structure across units, with grouping a smaller refinement.
The limits matter too: no rule beats the operators during an abrupt storm, entering units are poorly served by every rule, and the value of better commitment for storage planning depends on value streams we do not model.
