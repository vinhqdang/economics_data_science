\section{Day-ahead capacity commitment on the U.S.\ grid}\label{sec:app}

\subsection{Data}

\emph{Reading convention.} Every cost in this paper is an operating cost, so \emph{lower is better}; a ratio below 1 means a lower cost than the benchmark named in the table, and ``more expensive'' means worse. Gains, savings, storage values and the adjusted Rand index are the opposite: higher is better. Each caption states the direction.

We use the public U.S.\ Hourly Electric Grid Monitor (Form EIA-930; \citealp{EIA930}), downloaded on 29 September 2026: hourly demand, day-ahead demand forecast and total interchange for each balancing authority (BA) in the lower 48 states, 1 January 2019 to 26 September 2026 in UTC hours, excluding the fourteen regional aggregates.
Following \citet{Ruggles2020}, non-positive values and those outside $[0.3,3]$ times the preceding seven-day median are treated as missing (one-sided filter, applied to the official forecast against its own history) and gaps of up to three hours are interpolated.
We keep BAs with at least 97\% valid demand, at least 93\% valid official forecasts, mean demand above 50\,MW and a median absolute official forecast error below 15\% of median demand.
Forty-six BAs remain (Table~\ref{tab:bas} in Appendix~\ref{app:data}); they range from PJM (mean demand 92.5\,GW) to the City of Homestead (71\,MW), include all seven independent system operators and cover 97\% of lower-48 demand.
Ten mostly small BAs are dropped; 0.32\% of retained BA-hours are missing, and 0.6\% of official forecasts are replaced by the mean of the other candidates.

\subsection{Decision problem and cost calibration}

Before the day-ahead gate closure on day $d-1$, BA $i$ commits hourly capacity $q_{i,d,h}$, $h=0,\dots,23$, for the (UTC) operating day $d$.
Demand is observed only to the end of day $d-2$, since UTC day $d-1$ ends after any U.S.\ day-ahead deadline; the information set is demand and rule costs up to day $d-2$ plus the published forecast for day $d$.
This is conservative: using hours of day $d-1$ would slightly favour the data-driven candidates.
Excess commitment costs $o_i=1$ per MWh (the normalisation); shortfalls cost $u_i=\tau_i/(1-\tau_i)$, where the critical ratio $\tau_i$ reflects how easily the BA can lean on its neighbours.
As a proxy we use the 2019 range between the 5th and 95th percentiles of hourly interchange divided by mean demand, and let $\tau_i$ decline linearly in its rank from $0.95$ for the least variable BA to $0.80$ for the most.
Interchange variability is not transfer capability, so the proxy only orders BAs; Section~\ref{sec:robust} reports common critical ratios from 0.5 to 0.95 (Section~\ref{sec:global}: 0.99 for the global panel).
Almost islanded ERCOT receives $\tau=0.95$ ($u_i=19$); Chelan County PUD, in the interconnected Pacific Northwest, receives $\tau=0.80$ ($u_i=4$).

\subsection{Candidate rules}

Seven candidate forecasts are produced for every BA, day and hour:
(1) the official forecast;
(2) persistence (same hour of $d-2$);
(3) weekly persistence (same hour of $d-7$);
(4) the mean of the same hour over $d-2,\dots,d-8$;
(5) the mean of the same hour on $d-7$, $d-14$ and $d-21$;
(6) a BA-and-hour-specific linear regression on the $d-2$ and $d-7$ values, the official forecast and weekday dummies, on a rolling 56-day window refitted weekly; and
(7) a global LightGBM model \citep{Ke2017} trained on lags, calendar variables and the official forecast, all scaled by the BA's recent mean demand, refitted every 28 days on the preceding 365 days.
Each forecast $f^{(m)}$ becomes a candidate commitment with its own safety margin,
\[
q^{(m)}_{i,d,h}=f^{(m)}_{i,d,h}\,\big\{1+\widehat Q_{\tau_i}(e^{(m)}_i)\big\},
\]
where $\widehat Q_{\tau_i}(e^{(m)}_i)$ is the $\tau_i$-quantile of the relative errors $(y-f^{(m)})/\max\{f^{(m)},0.2\bar y_{i,t}\}$ of forecast $m$ over the 28 days ending at $d-2$; the 20\% floor on the BA's mean demand $\bar y_{i,t}$ over the week before day $t$ bounds the margin.
Given the 28-day margin window, the 56-day window cap and the two-day lag, candidates exist from February 2020 and evaluation runs from 1 May 2020 to 26 September 2026: 2,340 days, 335 weekly re-estimations and 25.8 billion MWh of demand.

\subsection{Competing methods}

Every seven days, at origin $d_0$, each method re-estimates its combination from the $W$ most recent days with known outcomes, $d_0-W-1,\dots,d_0-2$, $W\in\{7,14,28,56\}$ (24 hourly observations per BA per day), and commits for the next seven days.
Besides GDMA and soft GDMA ($G_{\max}=6$) we consider
the official-forecast policy (candidate~1 alone), the reference rule, a stylisation of actual commitment, which runs through market clearing;
equal weights over the seven candidates;
the best single candidate per BA;
pooled and per-BA decision-focused weights;
shrinkage of per-BA towards pooled weights (cf.\ \citealp{DieboldShin2019});
two-step clustering, which runs $k$-means on per-BA weight vectors and re-estimates pooled weights within clusters;
a quantile-regression combination of the seven forecasts at each BA's $\tau_i$ without the simplex constraint (\citealp{Wang2019cqra});
and two \emph{forecast-then-commit} methods, which estimate per-BA or grouped weights by minimising scaled squared forecast error and then add the $\tau_i$-quantile of the combined forecast's relative errors over 28 days, isolating the value of estimating for the decision rather than for accuracy.
The number of clusters, $G$, $\kappa$ and the shrinkage intensity are chosen on one hold-out split in which every third day, counted back from the window's end, is a validation day.

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the main result.

\emph{First, deciding for the decision pays.}
Soft GDMA lowers the system-wide commitment cost by 13--17\% relative to the official forecasts (significant at the 1\% level for every window), and every decision-focused simplex combination by 11--17\%.
The forecast-then-commit methods save 7--15\% and are significantly more expensive than soft GDMA for every window, most at $W=7$ (0.930 and 0.882 against 0.867), as accuracy-optimal weights ignore the asymmetric costs.
The unconstrained quantile-regression combination is competitive only with long windows (0.846 at $W=56$) and poor with short ones (1.297 at $W=7$), where dropping the simplex costs more in variance than it gains.
Equal weights are worst, raising cost by 69\%.

\emph{Second, neither extreme is right.}
Per-BA weights are costly with short windows (0.890 at $W=7$, 4.0\% above pooled weights), pooled weights with long windows (0.840 at $W=56$, 1.4\% above the best grouped rule), and their ranking reverses between $W=14$ and $W=28$, the bias--variance trade-off behind GDMA.

\emph{Third, grouping helps, but the three grouped rules are statistically indistinguishable.}
For $W\ge14$ the three cheapest methods are two-step clustering, GDMA and soft GDMA, and at $W=28$ they form the 90\% model confidence set of \citet{Hansen2011mcs} (marked $\dagger$ in Table~\ref{tab:main}), which excludes shrinkage (MCS $p$-value 0.03) and all other rules.
They differ by less than 0.2\% at $W=28$ and $W=56$ and about 0.5\% at $W=14$; two-step clustering is marginally cheapest, and at $W=7$ pooled weights are cheapest (0.856, against 0.859--0.867 for the grouped rules).
Soft GDMA beats the official forecast for the largest share of BAs (63\%, against 57--59\% for the other grouped rules and 54\% for shrinkage), and the one-standard-error rule lowers GDMA's cost at $W=7$ (0.864 against 0.874 for the hold-out minimum).
Which grouped variant is used matters less than using cross-sectional structure.

\emph{Fourth, the savings are economically meaningful.}
At $W=28$ the official-forecast policy costs 0.0779 units per MWh of demand and soft GDMA 0.0651, a saving of 0.0128.
With roughly 4.0 billion MWh of annual demand (25.8 billion over the evaluation period) and idle capacity valued at an illustrative \$10 per MWh, this is roughly \$500 million a year under the stylised cost model.
The saving is not universal: 63\% of BAs are better off than under their own official forecast (last column of Table~\ref{tab:main}), and the official forecast is better for the rest.

\subsection{Segments}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/segments.pdf}
\caption{Left: number of segments selected by GDMA in each weekly re-estimation ($W=28$). Right: among weeks with $G\ge2$, share of weeks in which two BAs share a segment.}\label{fig:segments}

GDMA uses one segment in 16\% of the weeks and two to four in two thirds of them (Figure~\ref{fig:segments}, left); soft GDMA's average shrinkage towards the segment rule is only $\kappa=0.14$, so it mostly refines unit-level weights.
The co-membership matrix (right) shows two stable segments.
The first has 16 BAs, among them PJM and CAISO, and weights the hourly regression 0.38, LightGBM 0.39 and the official forecast 0.20.
The second has 29 BAs, including MISO and ERCOT, and puts 0.44 on the official forecast; the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power often forms its own segment.
The segments have similar average critical ratios (0.886 and 0.868) and do not follow geography or market design; both mix ISOs and vertically integrated utilities from the East and West.
They differ in how much the operator's forecast adds to what recent demand reveals.

In Table~\ref{tab:events}, during Winter Storm Uri and the 2023 summer heat, the decision-focused combinations cost 4--7\% and 16--19\% less than the official-forecast policy.
For Uri, ERCOT, and on two days SPP and MISO, shed load \citep{FERCNERC2021} and EIA-930 reports served demand, so anticipating true demand is scored as over-commitment; Section~\ref{sec:global} repeats the analysis without the load-shed BA-days.
During Winter Storm Elliott the operators' forecasts beat every candidate rule over the six days and every combination is 17--30\% more expensive than the official policy, as expected for an abrupt cold snap with large forced outages \citep{FERCNERC2023} that weights learned from earlier weeks cannot anticipate; Section~\ref{sec:global} tests whether weather-driven candidates narrow this gap.

Table~\ref{tab:tau} replaces the calibrated ratios by a common $\tau$, from the symmetric $\tau=0.5$ to the highly asymmetric $\tau=0.95$.
Savings change little (soft GDMA saves 17--19\%) and neither does the ranking: two-step clustering is cheapest in every column, soft GDMA and GDMA follow within 0.5\%, then shrinkage, per-BA and pooled weights.
The advantage over the better forecast-then-commit method grows with the asymmetry, from 1.4\% at $\tau=0.5$ to 2.8\% at $\tau=0.95$, as the best combination for the decision departs further from the most accurate one.
