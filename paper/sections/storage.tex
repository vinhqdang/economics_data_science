\section{Where to install batteries}\label{sec:storage}

We now solve the planning problem~\eqref{eq:siting} on the global panel.
For each commitment rule we take its out-of-sample hourly shortfalls and surpluses ($W=28$, eleven candidates with GFS and GEFS weather forecasts, day-ahead gate-closure information), place a four-hour battery with 85\% round-trip efficiency in every grid area, and compute the annual value of battery power on a grid from 0 to 15\% of the area's mean demand.
Costs are converted to money by valuing one MWh of idle committed capacity at an illustrative \$10, so a shortfall costs between \$40 and \$190 per MWh depending on the area's critical ratio; all dollar figures scale proportionally.
The battery is dispatched greedily against the given commitment path, charging from idle committed capacity and discharging into shortfalls; the commitment is not re-optimised with the battery in place, which would lower its value.
The exercise values a single stream, cover for day-ahead commitment errors, and ignores arbitrage, adequacy, renewable firming and network deferral, which dominate real storage decisions.
It illustrates how the operational rule changes the value and location of storage and is not a siting recommendation; annual values average over each area's own evaluation period.

\subsection{Batteries do not pay for themselves on forecast errors alone}

The first increment of battery power is worth \$31,000--\$45,000 per MW-year in the median area of each continent (depending on the rule), and at most \$73,000 (in the PJM interconnection).
Annualised costs of four-hour batteries are well above this range (we use \$100,000--\$200,000 per MW-year), so the cost-optimal battery size for covering commitment errors alone is zero in every area and for every rule.
Storage is deployed for other reasons; covering commitment errors is a by-product.
The planning question is therefore not whether but \emph{where} to place the storage that energy-transition programmes already mandate, and how that depends on how operators commit.

Without storage (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}, Table~\ref{tab:storage}), soft GDMA commits the five continents' capacity for \$11.3 billion a year under the stylised cost model, against \$12.3 billion for the reference forecaster, a difference of \$1.0 billion or 8\%.
Adding batteries lowers every curve: the reference rule needs 34\,GW of optimally sited batteries to reach the cost that soft GDMA achieves without any, forecast-then-commit needs 17\,GW and per-area decision-focused weights 5\,GW, while pooled weights and plain GDMA are as cheap as soft GDMA without storage in this metric.
The battery-equivalent is not the value of the better rule: 34\,GW would cost far more than the \$1.0 billion difference in commitment cost.
The point is the interaction: the better the rule, the less is left for storage to absorb, and a planner who values storage by today's errors overstates it.

\subsection{The siting decision depends on the commitment rule}

Table~\ref{tab:storage} also shows how a 10\,GW programme should be split.
Under soft GDMA, 4.3\,GW go to North America, 2.6\,GW to Europe, 0.7\,GW to Oceania and 2.5\,GW to Asia, and Africa receives almost nothing.
Using the reference forecaster's error profile instead, North America receives 5.1\,GW, about 19\% more, because the U.S.\ balancing authorities' own forecasts leave the largest shortfalls; forecast-then-commit would send 5.0\,GW there.

Siting on estimated value curves also carries estimation risk.
Table~\ref{tab:oos} sites batteries on the first half of the evaluation period and values them on the second: soft GDMA's own profile captures about three quarters of the infeasible oracle's value at 10\,GW (\$247 million of \$336 million), and the reference rule's profile captures 6\% less than that at 10\,GW and 8\% less at 20\,GW.
The two sources of loss, the choice of rule and the estimation of the profile, are of similar size for programmes of 5--10\,GW.

Efficient siting concentrates batteries where the monetary value is highest, so in small programmes some continents receive nothing: with 1\,GW, the worst-served continent's relative benefit is zero (Table~\ref{tab:fairsiting}).
Floors proportional to each continent's share of demand, the ``fair share'' rule, raise it only slightly (from 1.4\% to 1.7\% of its commitment cost with 10\,GW) and give up 3--7\% of the programme's value.
Maximin siting, which directs every increment to the continent that has gained least so far, raises it much more, to 2.8\% with 10\,GW and from zero to 0.3\% with 1\,GW, at a price of fairness of 7--9\%.
Fairness in storage siting is therefore not free here: protecting the worst-served continent costs between a thirtieth and a tenth of the programme's value, and only for one value stream and with the continent as the unit of fairness.
