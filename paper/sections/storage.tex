\section{Where to install batteries}\label{sec:storage}

We now solve the planning problem~\eqref{eq:siting} on the global panel.
For each commitment rule we take its out-of-sample hourly shortfalls and surpluses ($W=28$, nine candidates with GFS weather forecasts), place a four-hour battery with 85\% round-trip efficiency in every grid area, and compute the annual value of battery power on a grid from 0 to 15\% of the area's mean demand.
Costs are converted to money by valuing one MWh of idle committed capacity at \$10, so that a shortfall costs between \$40 and \$190 per MWh depending on the area's critical ratio; the value of battery power scales proportionally with this assumption.

\subsection{Batteries do not pay for themselves on forecast errors alone}

The first increment of battery power is worth \$37,000--\$46,000 per MW-year in the median area of each continent (depending on the commitment rule), and at most \$84,000 (in Great Britain).
Annualised costs of four-hour batteries are well above this range (we use \$100,000--\$200,000 per MW-year), so the cost-optimal battery size for covering day-ahead commitment errors alone is zero in every area and for every commitment rule.
Storage is deployed for arbitrage, capacity adequacy and the integration of renewables, and covering commitment errors is a by-product.
The relevant planning question is therefore not whether, but \emph{where}, to place the storage that energy-transition programmes already mandate, and how that choice depends on the way operators commit.

Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} and Table~\ref{tab:storage} give the central managerial result.
Without storage, soft GDMA commits the five continents' capacity for \$8.00 billion a year, against \$9.59 billion for the single best forecaster.
Adding batteries lowers every curve, but the curve of the single best forecaster never reaches soft GDMA's starting point: even 60\,GW of optimally sited batteries leave it at \$8.11 billion.
Relative to the standard ways of combining forecasts, soft GDMA is worth 5\,GW (per-area weights) and 7\,GW (forecast-then-commit and pooled weights) of batteries.
At \$150,000 per MW-year, 5--7\,GW of batteries cost \$0.75--1.05 billion a year; the re-weighting of existing forecasts that replaces them costs essentially nothing.

\subsection{The siting decision depends on the commitment rule}

Table~\ref{tab:storage} also shows how a 10\,GW programme should be distributed.
Under soft GDMA, 2.6\,GW go to North America, 2.5\,GW to Europe, 0.5\,GW to Oceania and 4.4\,GW to Asia, and Africa receives almost nothing.
If the planner instead uses the error profile of the single best forecaster, North America receives 3.7\,GW, about 40\% more, because the status quo leaves U.S.\ operators with the largest shortfalls; the forecast-then-commit rule would send 4.2\,GW there.
Those batteries would be in the wrong place once operations improve.

Table~\ref{tab:siting} quantifies the cost of that mistake.
When operations follow soft GDMA, a 1\,GW programme sited with the status-quo error profile delivers \$39 million a year instead of \$46 million, a 15\% loss; the loss is 7\% at 5\,GW, 3\% at 10\,GW and 2\% at 20\,GW, because large programmes eventually reach every area with sizeable shortfalls.
Small, early programmes---the typical first phase of a national storage strategy---are therefore the most sensitive to the operational rule assumed at the planning stage.
The implication for planners is sequencing: fix how forecasts are combined first, and site storage on the residual risk that remains.

Efficient siting concentrates batteries where the value in money is highest, and in small programmes some continents receive nothing: with 1\,GW, Africa's relative benefit is zero (Table~\ref{tab:fairsiting}).
Two fair rules give different answers.
Floors proportional to each continent's share of demand, the ``fair share'' rule often used in practice, cost 0.7--1.5\% of the programme's value and \emph{lower} the relative benefit of the worst-served continent at 5\,GW and above, because they move batteries from small systems with large shortfalls, such as those in Oceania, to large systems where each MW achieves little in relative terms.
Maximin siting, which directs every increment to the continent that has gained least so far, raises the worst-served continent's relative benefit at every budget---for example from 3.8\% to 4.3\% of its commitment cost with 10\,GW, which doubles Africa's allocation, and from zero to 0.5\% with 1\,GW---and gives up only 0.4--1.2\% of the programme's value.
For planners the lesson is that fairness in storage siting is cheap if it is defined by who benefits, and can be counter-productive if it is defined by who consumes.
