\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Large infrastructure systems are managed through many local decision units that commit resources before uncertainty resolves.
The U.S.\ electric grid is an extreme example.
Its lower 48 states are operated by several dozen balancing authorities---independent system operators, federal power marketing agencies, investor-owned and municipal utilities---each of which must, every day, commit enough generating capacity to meet the next day's hourly demand.
Committing too little forces expensive real-time purchases, the activation of reserves or, in the worst case, curtailed load; committing too much means paying for capacity that sits idle.
The cost of a shortfall is not the same everywhere: an operator embedded in a dense web of interconnections can import from its neighbours, while an isolated one cannot.
Winter Storm Uri in February 2021, when the weakly interconnected Texas system shed load for days while better-connected neighbours coped, showed how much this asymmetry matters \citep{Busby2021}.

Every balancing authority publishes a day-ahead demand forecast, and a planner can build many more commitment rules from persistence, calendar profiles, statistical models and machine learning.
None dominates everywhere, which makes combining them attractive.
How to combine them is less obvious.
The forecast-combination and model-averaging literature, starting with \citet{BatesGranger1969} and developed into an asymptotic theory by \citet{Hansen2007}, \citet{HansenRacine2012}, \citet{ZhangWanZou2013} and \citet{ZhangLiu2023}, offers two standard options when there are many series.
One can estimate combination weights for each series separately, which respects heterogeneity but produces noisy weights when histories are short---the root of the ``forecast-combination puzzle'' that simple averages often beat estimated weights \citep{SmithWallis2009,Claeskens2016}.
Or one can pool all series and estimate one set of weights, which is precise but imposes the same policy on units that face different conditions.
Both options also judge a combination by its statistical accuracy, even though the planner is judged by the cost of the resulting decisions.
The operations-management literature has shown that learning directly for the decision---the ``decision-focused'' or ``predict-then-optimise'' perspective---can be much better than first forecasting accurately and then optimising \citep{BanRudin2019,BertsimasKallus2020,ElmachtoubGrigas2022}, but this literature has not addressed the combination of existing forecasts across many heterogeneous units.

This paper proposes \emph{grouped decision-focused model averaging} (GDMA), a new method designed for this setting.
GDMA assumes that the decision units fall into a small number of latent segments whose members are best served by the same combination of candidate rules, and estimates the segments and the segment-specific weights jointly by minimising the realised system-wide decision cost.
The number of segments is chosen by a time-ordered hold-out rule.
Each unit keeps its own cost parameters and its own candidate safety margins, so segment members share a \emph{rule} but not a commitment level.
Pooled weights and unit-specific weights are the two extreme cases ($G=1$ and $G=N$).

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Method.} We formulate the joint estimation of latent segments and combination weights under an asymmetric, unit-specific decision loss, and give a $k$-means-type algorithm with regret-based seeding in which each step is a small convex program on the simplex (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
To the best of our knowledge neither the model-averaging literature, nor the decision-focused learning literature, nor the latent-group panel literature \citep{BonhommeManresa2015,Su2016} has proposed this estimator.
\item \textbf{Theory.} We prove a finite-sample oracle inequality that splits the estimation error into a term for learning memberships ($\log G/T$ per unit) and a term for learning weights ($GM\log(NT)/(NT)$), which formalises why grouping beats unit-specific weights when histories are short.
We show that GDMA is asymptotically optimal and, under a segment structure, attains the risk of the infeasible benchmark in which every unit knows its own optimal weights; that it recovers the segments with probability approaching one; and that on this event it coincides exactly with an estimator told the segments (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
\item \textbf{Evidence.} In Monte Carlo experiments GDMA is the most cost-effective feasible method when segments exist, loses little when they do not, and recovers the segments far more accurately than clustering noisy unit-level weights (Section~\ref{sec:sim}).
\item \textbf{Application.} Using the complete public record of hourly demand and official day-ahead forecasts of 46 U.S.\ balancing authorities from 2019 to September 2026 (EIA Form~930), we evaluate day-ahead capacity commitment over more than six years of out-of-sample operation, including Winter Storms Uri and Elliott and the summer heat waves of 2023--2025 (Section~\ref{sec:app}).
The results quantify how much system-wide commitment cost a planner could save by combining forecasts in a decision-focused, segment-aware way, and which operators should share a policy.
\end{enumerate}

The paper speaks directly to the theme of this special issue: it develops a statistical method motivated by a concrete management problem, establishes its theoretical guarantees, and demonstrates its value for a decision of national scale using new, openly available data.
