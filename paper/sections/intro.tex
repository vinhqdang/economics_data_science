\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Electricity systems consist of many local decision units that commit resources before uncertainty resolves.
Every day, each balancing authority and market operator must commit enough capacity for the next day's hourly demand: too little forces expensive real-time purchases, reserve activation or load shedding, too much pays for idle capacity.
Shortfalls cost less for a well-interconnected operator, which can import from neighbours, than for an isolated one.
In Winter Storm Uri in February 2021 the weakly interconnected Texas grid shed load for days, while neighbours with more import capability shed much less \citep{Busby2021,FERCNERC2021}.
Meanwhile, governments are funding large battery programmes, and planners must decide where the batteries go.

Forecasts are plentiful: official day-ahead forecasts, persistence and calendar rules, statistical and machine-learning models, and foundation models that forecast any series zero-shot \citep{Ansari2024chronos}.
None dominates everywhere, so combining them is attractive.
Model averaging, from \citet{BatesGranger1969} to \citet{Hansen2007}, \citet{HansenRacine2012}, \citet{ZhangWanZou2013} and \citet{ZhangLiu2023}, offers two options for many series.
Weights can be estimated series by series, which respects heterogeneity but is noisy when histories are short---the root of the ``forecast-combination puzzle'' that simple averages often beat estimated weights \citep{SmithWallis2009,Claeskens2016}---or one set can be estimated for all series, which is precise but imposes one rule on systems facing different conditions.
A third option, a network mapping features to weights \citep{MonteroManso2020,Gong2026moe}, costs thousands of parameters and weights that are hard to audit.
All three typically judge a combination by accuracy, whereas the planner is judged by decision cost; learning directly for the decision can be much better than forecasting accurately and then optimising \citep{BanRudin2019,BertsimasKallus2020,ElmachtoubGrigas2022}.

This paper proposes \emph{grouped decision-focused model averaging} (GDMA) and uses it to answer three management questions: how should grid operators on five continents combine their forecasts into day-ahead capacity commitments, who gains and who loses from a common planning rule, and where should new batteries be installed?
GDMA assumes that decision units fall into a small number of latent segments whose members are best served by the same combination of candidate rules, and estimates segments and segment-specific weights jointly by minimising the realised system-wide decision cost, in money, with each unit's own cost asymmetry.
A soft version shrinks each unit's weights towards its segment's, and the number of segments and the shrinkage intensity are chosen by a time-ordered hold-out.
Pooled weights, unit-specific weights and shrinkage towards pooled weights are special cases, and each commitment is a convex combination of commitments the operator already understands.
A second stage allocates a storage budget across grid areas using the residual shortfall risk of the learned rule.

We make five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Method.} We formulate joint learning of latent segments and combination weights under an asymmetric, unit-specific decision loss, give a $k$-means-type algorithm with regret-space seeding in which every step is a small convex program, and link it to a storage-siting problem solved exactly by a greedy algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
To our knowledge, no model-averaging, decision-focused or latent-group panel work \citep{BonhommeManresa2015,Su2016} proposes this estimator.
\item \textbf{Learning theory.} We prove a finite-sample oracle inequality that separates the cost of learning memberships ($\log G/T$ per unit) from the cost of learning weights ($GM\log(NT)/(NT)$); GDMA is asymptotically optimal, attains under a segment structure the risk of the infeasible benchmark in which every unit knows its own optimal weights, recovers the segments with probability approaching one, and then coincides exactly with an estimator told the segments (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
\item \textbf{Evidence on five continents.} With information available at the day-ahead gate closure, on 46 U.S.\ balancing authorities with their official forecasts (Section~\ref{sec:app}) and on a global panel of 125 grid areas in North America, Europe, Oceania, Asia and Africa (Section~\ref{sec:global}), soft GDMA lowers the stylised out-of-sample commitment cost by 13--17\% relative to the official forecasts in the United States and by 12\% worldwide relative to a reference forecaster, beats accuracy-based combination by 2--3 points in the United States (at $W=28$) and 8--10 points worldwide, and beats a decision-focused neural mixture-of-experts gate by seven percentage points worldwide. Decision-focused shrinkage and two-step clustering come close and a model confidence set cannot separate the grouped variants, so the evidence favours decision-focused combination with cross-sectional structure, not grouping alone. Ensemble weather information roughly halves, but does not close, the gap to the operators' forecasts during Winter Storm Elliott, the most abrupt event in the sample.
\item \textbf{Fairness.} We audit how gains are distributed and propose a fair variant that counts every area's relative improvement equally. Soft GDMA makes 6\% of areas worse off than their own best candidate, against 24\% for pooled weights and 42\% for the neural gate, shows no detectable gradient with national or state income, and its fair variant halves the share of areas made worse off at no cost in total (Section~\ref{sec:fairaudit}).
\item \textbf{Storage siting.} In a stylised storage model, better commitment rules reduce what storage can add: the reference rule needs 34\,GW of optimally sited batteries to reach the cost that soft GDMA achieves without any. Siting on the wrong rule's error profile loses only 2--4\% of a programme's value, and maximin-fair siting helps the worst-served continent at a price of fairness of 7--9\% (Section~\ref{sec:storage}).
\end{enumerate}

The paper fits the theme of this special issue in both directions: a statistical learning method with guarantees, motivated by a management problem, and a continental-scale planning question answered with open data.
Replication code is in a public repository (see the data availability statement).
