\section{Capacity commitment on five continents}\label{sec:global}

\subsection{A global panel of grid areas}

We assemble public hourly demand data for 125 grid areas on five continents (Table~\ref{tab:globaldata}).
\emph{North America} is represented by the 46 U.S.\ balancing authorities of Section~\ref{sec:app} (EIA Form~930, 2019--2026), the only systems in the panel that publish their own day-ahead forecasts.
\emph{Europe} contributes 33 national systems from the Energy-Charts platform of Fraunhofer ISE \citep{EnergyCharts}, 2018--2026, including cross-border trading flows.
\emph{Oceania} contributes the five regions of Australia's National Electricity Market \citep{AEMO}, 2018--2026.
\emph{Asia} contributes the 31 provinces of mainland China for 2018 \citep{ChinaLoad2023}, the four grid areas of Taiwan for 2018 to mid-2022 \citep{TaiwanLoad2023} and the five regions of Thailand for 2023--2024 \citep{ThaiLoad2025}.
\emph{Africa} is represented by Algeria (Sonelgaz), 2018 to January 2020 \citep{AlgeriaLoad2023}.
All series are converted to hourly UTC, cleaned as in Section~\ref{sec:app}, and placed on a common calendar from 1 January 2018 to 26 September 2026.
Together the areas have an average demand of 1.66\,TW---North America 455\,GW, Europe 339\,GW, Asia 839\,GW, Oceania 21\,GW and Africa 7\,GW---roughly half of world electricity demand, although not all areas are observed at the same time.

The panel is deliberately unbalanced.
Systems in Asia and Africa enter and leave, and several have only one or two years of data, which is typical of the data situation in emerging economies; an area contributes to estimation and evaluation only on days with data.
Public hourly data for other large African and Asian systems (South Africa, India, Japan, South Korea) exist but could not be retrieved without registration or were not reachable from our computing environment; we discuss this limitation in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Candidates, costs and competitors}

Every area receives nine candidate forecasts: the seven of Section~\ref{sec:app}; a zero-shot forecast from the pretrained time-series foundation model Chronos-Bolt \citep{Ansari2024chronos}, which receives the previous 28 days of hourly demand and returns a 24-hour forecast without any fine-tuning; and a weather-aware global LightGBM model.
The weather model adds to the LightGBM features the hourly 2-m temperature of the operating day, its daily mean, minimum and maximum, heating and cooling degrees (base 18\,$^\circ$C) and the previous day's mean temperature.
Temperatures come from NASA POWER \citep{NASAPOWER}, which provides MERRA-2-based hourly data for any location without registration; each area is represented by the average of one to five load centres (169 locations in total, for example Dallas, Houston and San Antonio for ERCOT, or Paris, Lyon, Marseille and Lille for France).
Realised temperature would be a perfect weather forecast, an \emph{ex post} forecast in the terminology of \citet{HongFan2016}.
In the main specification we therefore perturb the operating-day temperatures with a serially correlated error of standard deviation 2\,$^\circ$C, drawn independently for every area and day, so that the candidate carries the kind of uncertainty an operator's day-ahead weather forecast would have; the model with exact temperatures is reported as an upper bound on the value of weather information.
The LightGBM model is trained jointly on all 125 areas with continent and area identifiers and is refitted every 56 days on the preceding year.
Outside the United States no official forecast exists; there the ``official'' candidate is set equal to the LightGBM forecast, which leaves the set of attainable combinations unchanged, and the benchmark policy is therefore the \emph{single best forecaster}: the operator's forecast where one is published and the global LightGBM model elsewhere.
Over the evaluation period the mean absolute errors, scaled by mean demand, are 3.5\% for the weather model (3.5\% with exact temperatures), 3.6\% for LightGBM, 3.8\% for Chronos and for the hourly regression, 4.5\% for the official/LightGBM candidate and 6--8\% for the naive rules; the foundation model is thus competitive with a model trained on the data themselves.

Critical ratios follow the flexibility rule of Section~\ref{sec:app} wherever interchange is measured (the United States, 31 European countries, and the isolated island grid of Taiwan, whose flexibility is zero); the remaining areas receive the midpoint $\tau=0.875$.
Section~\ref{sec:robust} and Table~\ref{tab:globaltau} in the appendix show that a common $\tau$ gives the same ranking.

Besides the benchmarks of Section~\ref{sec:app} we add a deep-learning competitor, a \emph{decision-focused neural gate}.
It is a two-hidden-layer perceptron (6,920 parameters) that maps each area's features at the weekly origin---each candidate's recent decision cost and forecast error over the previous 7 and 28 days, the area's critical ratio, size and continent, and the season---into combination weights through a softmax, as in mixture-of-experts model averaging \citep{Gong2026moe}.
It is trained end to end on the same smoothed newsvendor cost, on the realised outcomes of the previous 52 weeks, and re-trained every four weeks.
Unlike GDMA, it can let weights vary continuously with observed conditions; like GDMA, it only uses information available at the time of each decision.

Table~\ref{tab:global} shows that the results of the U.S.\ analysis hold on every continent.
Worldwide, soft GDMA lowers the commitment cost by 18.0\% relative to the single best forecaster, and its fair variant, discussed below, by 18.4\%.
Soft GDMA is significantly cheaper, at the 1\% level, than every competitor except its own fair variant, which is cheaper still, and the two forecast-then-commit methods, which are 11\% more expensive but whose daily costs are so volatile that the test is inconclusive; its margin over the closest competitors, shrinkage and two-step clustering, is small (0.5--0.6\%) but statistically clear.
One of the two GDMA variants has the lowest cost in Europe, Asia and Africa, and soft GDMA is tied with shrinkage in North America; in Oceania, with only five regions, the per-area, forecast-then-commit and neural-gate rules are 0.6--0.9\% cheaper than fair soft GDMA.

Three comparisons stand out.
First, decision-focused combination again dominates forecast-then-commit: combining the same forecasts for accuracy and adding a quantile margin saves only 9\% worldwide and \emph{raises} cost in Asia by 10--12\%, where short histories make the estimated margins of the combined forecasts unreliable.
Second, the neural gate is clearly worse than the simple decision-focused rules (0.916 against 0.819 worldwide) despite its flexibility; it is competitive only in Oceania.
Its weights are learned from a year of past weeks and transfer poorly to weeks in which the relative performance of the candidates has shifted, whereas GDMA re-estimates a few interpretable rules every week.
We did not tune the network extensively, so the comparison should be read as evidence that flexibility is not what this problem needs, not as a statement about the best achievable neural design.
Third, better candidates and better combination are complements.
The weather-aware model alone costs 0.941 of the single best forecaster, and adding it to the candidate set lowers the cost of soft GDMA from 0.828 to 0.819 (Table~\ref{tab:specs}); combining remains 13\% cheaper than the best single weather-aware model.
With exact temperatures---a perfect weather forecast---every decision-focused and forecast-then-commit method becomes a further 0.7--1.1 points cheaper and the ranking is unchanged, so the conclusions do not depend on how accurate the weather forecast is assumed to be.
Weather information also narrows the gap to the operators during Winter Storm Elliott: over those six days soft GDMA's excess cost over the U.S.\ official forecasts falls from 7.7\% without the weather candidate to 3.0\% with it, and plain GDMA is at parity (1.011).
The ranking is stable across windows and critical ratios (Tables~\ref{tab:globalwindows} and~\ref{tab:globaltau} in the appendix).

\paragraph{New and data-poor systems.}
Fifty-two areas enter the panel after the start of the evaluation: the 46 U.S.\ balancing authorities in January 2019, the five Thai regions in January 2023 and one European system.
During their first eight weeks each of them has only a few weeks of history (Table~\ref{tab:entry}).
Pooled weights, which impose the rule learned from other systems, are 23\% more expensive than the single best forecaster in this phase, and the neural gate, whose features are not yet informative, is 63\% more expensive.
The three GDMA variants are the cheapest methods for entering areas (0.962--0.969), ahead of two-step clustering (0.976) and per-area weights (0.991): a new system borrows the rule of the segment it resembles after a few weeks, which is the mechanism behind the $\log G/T$ term of Theorem~\ref{thm:oracle}.

\paragraph{Segments across continents.}
GDMA uses more than one segment in 73\% of the weekly re-estimations (two segments in 37\%, three in 19\%), much more often than in the U.S.\ panel, and soft GDMA shrinks towards the segment rule with an average intensity of $\kappa=0.70$.
Segments are not continents.
Among weeks with several segments, two European systems share a segment 65\% of the time and two U.S.\ balancing authorities 52\% of the time, but European, Oceanian and African systems are grouped together 63--73\% of the time, and North American areas are grouped with European ones only 27\% of the time.
The segmentation therefore does not follow geography; it is consistent with differences in which forecasting systems work---notably whether an operator's own forecast is available and informative---which a planner could not have specified in advance.

Table~\ref{tab:fairness} asks how the gains are distributed.
Methods that impose a common rule or learn a flexible global map spread their gains unevenly: pooled weights make 13\% of the areas worse off than their own single best forecaster, and the neural gate and the forecast-then-commit methods 25--33\%; for the neural gate the median area in the worst-served continent loses 4\%.
Soft GDMA makes only 4\% of areas worse off and has the best worst-served continent among the efficiency-oriented methods, and fair soft GDMA improves on every count (3\% harmed, the tightest distribution with a 90th percentile of 0.957, and a worst-served continent at 0.875).
A gap remains between data-rich and data-poor systems: data-poor areas save 14\% under the GDMA variants against 18--19\% for data-rich ones, and per-area weights, which have little data to work with for these systems, save only 10\%.

Strikingly, fairness costs nothing here.
Fair soft GDMA, which counts every area's relative improvement equally, is also the cheapest method in total (0.816 against 0.819, significant at the 1\% level).
Weighting by relative cost prevents the few largest systems from dictating the segment rules, and the rules that serve the many smaller systems well turn out to generalise better out of sample---consistent with the segment-structure argument of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, under which fair and efficient criteria share their minimiser.
