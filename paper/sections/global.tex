\section{Capacity commitment on five continents}\label{sec:global}

\subsection{A global panel of grid areas}

We assemble public hourly demand data for 125 grid areas on five continents (Table~\ref{tab:globaldata}).
\emph{North America} is represented by the 46 U.S.\ balancing authorities of Section~\ref{sec:app} (EIA Form~930, 2019--2026), the only systems for which the operators' own day-ahead forecasts are part of the data; European transmission system operators and AEMO also publish day-ahead forecasts, but not through the sources we use, so outside the United States the reference rule is a model forecast (Section~\ref{sec:globalcand}).
\emph{Europe} contributes 33 national systems from the Energy-Charts platform of Fraunhofer ISE \citep{EnergyCharts}, 2018--2026, including cross-border trading flows.
\emph{Oceania} contributes the five regions of Australia's National Electricity Market \citep{AEMO}, 2018--2026.
\emph{Asia} contributes the 31 provinces of mainland China for 2018 \citep{ChinaLoad2023}, the four grid areas of Taiwan for 2018 to mid-2022 \citep{TaiwanLoad2023} and the five regions of Thailand for 2023--2024 \citep{ThaiLoad2025}.
\emph{Africa} is represented by Algeria (Sonelgaz), 2018 to January 2020 \citep{AlgeriaLoad2023}.
All series are converted to hourly UTC, cleaned as in Section~\ref{sec:app}, and placed on a common calendar from 1 January 2018 to 26 September 2026.
The average demands of the areas sum to 1.66\,TW---North America 455\,GW, Europe 339\,GW, Asia 839\,GW, Oceania 21\,GW and Africa 7\,GW---but the areas are observed in different periods (the Chinese provinces only in 2018), so this is not the demand served at any one time.

The panel is deliberately unbalanced.
Systems in Asia and Africa enter and leave, and several have only one or two years of data, which is typical of the data situation in emerging economies; an area contributes to estimation and evaluation only on days with data.
Public hourly data for other large African and Asian systems (South Africa, India, Japan, South Korea) exist but could not be retrieved without registration or were not reachable from our computing environment; we discuss this limitation in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Candidates, costs and competitors}\label{sec:globalcand}

Every area receives eleven candidate forecasts: the seven of Section~\ref{sec:app}; a zero-shot forecast from the pretrained time-series foundation model Chronos-Bolt (the small model; \citealp{Ansari2024chronos}), which receives the 28 days of hourly demand known at the decision time, forecasts the next 48 hours and contributes the last 24 of them, without any fine-tuning; and three weather-aware global LightGBM models.
Chronos was pretrained on public time series that include electricity-load data, so some of our series or periods may have been part of its training corpus; its forecasts should be read with this in mind.
Outside the United States candidate~0, the \emph{reference forecaster}, is the global LightGBM forecast; it is a strong model-based rule, not the best single candidate, which is reported separately as the per-area best single candidate chosen in each window.
The temperature model adds to the LightGBM features the hourly 2-m temperature of the operating day, its daily mean, minimum and maximum, heating and cooling degrees (base 18\,$^\circ$C) and the previous day's mean temperature.
Temperatures come from NASA POWER \citep{NASAPOWER}, which provides MERRA-2-based hourly data for any location without registration; each area is represented by the average of one to five load centres (169 locations in total, for example Dallas, Houston and San Antonio for ERCOT, or Paris, Lyon, Marseille and Lille for France).
Realised temperature would be a perfect weather forecast, an \emph{ex post} forecast in the terminology of \citet{HongFan2016}.
The weather models therefore use \emph{archived day-ahead forecasts} of NOAA's Global Forecast System \citep{GFSarchive}: for day $d$ we take the run initialised at 00~UTC on day $d-1$ and its 2-m temperature forecasts for lead times of 24 to 48 hours, interpolated to the load centres and to hourly resolution.
This run becomes available at about 04--05~UTC, before a 10:00-local gate closure in the Americas, Europe and Algeria but after it in East Asia and Australia, so the weather models use numerical weather forecasts only in the first group of areas.
Only the required fields are downloaded from each 1-degree forecast file, by byte range (18,846 temperature fields, 99.8\% retrieved).
The archive we use, NOAA's open-data archive of 1-degree GFS files, starts in January 2021 (longer archives exist, for example at NCEI and in the GEFSv12 reforecast, but were not used), so the weather models have forecasts from 2021 onwards and fall back on their other features before.
The previous day's temperature is not yet observed when the commitment is made; it is taken from the GFS forecast for day $d-1$ issued on day $d-2$.
Against realised temperatures the GFS forecasts have a root-mean-squared error of 2.3\,$^\circ$C (1.9\,$^\circ$C in Europe, 2.2\,$^\circ$C in North America, 2.7\,$^\circ$C in Asia).
A version of the temperature model with realised temperatures is reported as an upper bound on the value of temperature information.
The \emph{multi-variable model} adds, from the same GFS runs and lead times, the forecast 2-m relative humidity, 10-m wind speed and downward short-wave radiation at the surface (99.8\% of the files from 2021 retrieved), and from the GEFSv12 ensemble \citep{GEFSv12,GEFSarchive} the ensemble standard deviation of 2-m temperature at 0.5 degrees (available for every day from October 2020), each hourly and as daily means.
The spread measures how uncertain tomorrow's weather is, which a point forecast cannot convey, so the third weather candidate uses it for the commitment margin rather than for the point forecast: it takes the multi-variable forecast and multiplies its quantile margin by the ratio of the operating day's spread to its mean over the previous 28 days, clipped to $[0.5,3]$ (\emph{ensemble margin}).
The rule was motivated by the gap to the operators' forecasts in Winter Storm Elliott; its constants were chosen once and not tuned, but since it was designed with that event in view, and uses a raw, uncalibrated ensemble spread from a single archive, it should be read as exploratory.
On an average day the spread says little about the size of the error (rank correlation 0.04 across area-days, median 0.08 within areas), but it is large precisely on days of unusual weather.
Over the evaluation period the mean absolute errors, scaled by mean demand, are 3.5\% for the temperature and multi-variable models (3.5\% with exact temperatures), 3.6\% for LightGBM, 3.8\% for Chronos and for the hourly regression, 4.5\% for the official/LightGBM candidate and 6--8\% for the naive rules; the foundation model is thus competitive with a model trained on the data themselves.

Critical ratios follow the flexibility rule of Section~\ref{sec:app} wherever interchange is measured (the United States, 31 European countries, and the isolated island grid of Taiwan, whose flexibility is zero); the remaining areas receive the midpoint $\tau=0.875$.
Section~\ref{sec:robust} and Table~\ref{tab:globaltau} in the appendix show that a common $\tau$ gives the same ranking.

Besides the benchmarks of Section~\ref{sec:app} we add a deep-learning competitor, a \emph{decision-focused neural gate}.
It is a two-hidden-layer perceptron (NGPARAMS parameters with eleven candidates) that maps each area's features at the weekly origin---each candidate's recent decision cost and forecast error over the last 7 and 28 known days, the area's critical ratio, recent mean demand, share of days with data and continent, and the season---into combination weights through a softmax, as in mixture-of-experts model averaging \citep{Gong2026moe}.
It is trained end to end on the same smoothed newsvendor cost, on the up to 52 most recent weeks whose outcomes are fully observed, and re-trained every four weeks; the four most recent of these weeks serve for early stopping, and the weights of two independently initialised networks are averaged.
We did not search over architectures, so the gate represents a reasonably trained but untuned neural benchmark.
Unlike GDMA, it can let weights vary continuously with observed conditions; like GDMA, it only uses information available at the time of each decision.

Table~\ref{tab:global} shows that the main findings of the U.S.\ analysis carry over to the world panel, with smaller savings.
Worldwide, soft GDMA lowers the commitment cost by 11.6\% relative to the reference forecaster (the operators' official forecast in the United States, a global LightGBM forecast elsewhere) and by 4.2 percentage points relative to the best single candidate chosen in each window; its fair variant, discussed below, lowers it by 11.9\%.
At the 90\% level the model confidence set \citep{Hansen2011mcs} contains four methods---two-step clustering, GDMA, soft GDMA and fair soft GDMA---and excludes shrinkage towards pooled weights only narrowly ($p=0.098$), per-area and pooled weights equally narrowly, and the neural gate, the quantile-regression combination and the best single candidate more clearly.
Within the confidence set the differences are below 0.4\% and not statistically distinguishable; fair soft GDMA is the cheapest in total (0.881), significantly so against soft GDMA.
By continent the cheapest method is a GDMA variant in North America and Africa; in Europe and Oceania the neural gate is nominally cheapest (0.896 and 0.964, against 0.897 for fair soft GDMA in Europe and 0.972 for GDMA in Oceania), and in Asia pooled weights are cheapest (0.929 against 0.963--0.965), because most Asian areas have a single year or less of data.

Three comparisons stand out.
First, decision-focused combination on the simplex dominates forecast-then-commit and the unconstrained quantile-regression combination: combining the same forecasts for accuracy and adding a quantile margin saves only 1--3\% worldwide, and the quantile-regression combination is more expensive than the reference forecaster (1.016) because, with eleven highly collinear candidates and a few weeks of data, freeing the weights from the simplex costs more in variance than it gains in flexibility; both are particularly poor in Asia, where histories are short.
Second, a neural gate trained with early stopping and averaged over two initialisations is clearly worse than the simple decision-focused rules (0.956 against 0.884 worldwide), although it is competitive in Europe and Oceania.
We did not search over architectures, so this should be read as evidence that flexibility alone does not solve the problem, not as a statement about the best achievable neural design.
Third, better candidates and better combination are complements.
On their own the temperature model costs 0.965 of the reference forecaster, the multi-variable model 0.960 and its ensemble-margin version 0.981, the wider margins costing money on ordinary days; the global LightGBM model costs 0.988 and the zero-shot foundation model 1.434, which is much worse than with a one-day-ahead information set because it must now forecast two days ahead.
Adding the temperature model to the candidate set lowers the cost of soft GDMA from 0.906 to 0.892, and adding the two multi-variable candidates lowers it further to 0.884 (Table~\ref{tab:specs}); with realised temperatures in place of GFS forecasts in the temperature model, soft GDMA costs 0.876.
The ranking of the methods is the same for every candidate set, so better weather information helps all methods alike.
The ranking is also similar across estimation windows and critical ratios (Tables~\ref{tab:globalwindows} and~\ref{tab:globaltau} in the appendix).

\paragraph{Extreme events}
The U.S.\ analysis (Table~\ref{tab:events}) showed that during Winter Storm Elliott, the abrupt cold snap of 21--26 December 2022 \citep{FERCNERC2023}, the operators' own forecasts were better than every combination.
Table~\ref{tab:elliott} shows how much of that gap is a matter of weather information.
Without weather candidates soft GDMA is 20.0\% more expensive than the official forecasts over the six days; temperature forecasts from GFS reduce this to 14.7\%, and the multi-variable candidates, which add humidity, wind, radiation and the ensemble spread, to 9.5\%.
With realised temperatures in the temperature model the figure is 11.1\%, so the multi-variable set is not better than a perfect temperature forecast in any structural sense: the six days are too few to rank candidate sets reliably, and rankings among the combination rules also change with the set.
Excluding the BA-days on which TVA and Duke shed load \citep{FERCNERC2023}, which understate true demand, soft GDMA is 8.4\% more expensive.
The only rule that beats the operators during the storm is a single candidate: the multi-variable model with the ensemble margin, which is 7\% cheaper (0.929), because the GEFS spread rose on 23 December, the costliest day, and widened the margin that day by a median factor of 1.4.
The rule was designed after the gap in Elliott was known, precisely to address it, so this result is exploratory: it is a single candidate, evaluated on the single event that motivated it; over the whole period it is the most expensive of the three weather candidates (0.981), and the combination rules, whose weights are learned from the previous four weeks, do not give it enough weight to exploit it.
During Winter Storm Uri, soft GDMA is 11--13\% cheaper than the operators in every candidate set, and 11--13\% cheaper also when ERCOT's, SPP's and MISO's load-shed days are excluded.

A single storm is a weak basis for conclusions about extremes, so we also examine all extreme-temperature days by a rule fixed beforehand: the area-days on which the daily mean temperature is below its 2.5\% or above its 97.5\% quantile over the evaluation period (Table~\ref{tab:extremes}).
On cold days soft GDMA is 12\% cheaper than the reference forecaster worldwide (90\% interval 0.83--0.93, block bootstrap over days) and on hot days 19\% cheaper (0.79--0.84); in the United States 10\% and 25\% cheaper.
Elliott is therefore not typical of extreme weather in general; it is an abrupt cold snap accompanied by large forced outages, and the loss of the combination rules in that event reflects weights that cannot anticipate a regime change.

\paragraph{Tail risk}
The linear cost model hides the tail, so Table~\ref{tab:tail} reports the average of the costliest 5\% of days (CVaR$_{95}$) and the costliest week of the world's daily commitment cost.
Soft GDMA's costliest week is 16\% cheaper than the reference forecaster's (0.840) and its CVaR$_{95}$ is 9.5\% lower (0.905), so its saving is not bought with a heavier tail.
The forecast-then-commit and quantile-regression rules fail in the tail: their CVaR$_{95}$ is 55--63\% higher than the reference forecaster's and their costliest week is nine times as costly, mostly in the first week of January 2019, when the 46 U.S.\ balancing authorities enter the panel with a few days of data; even without each area's first eight weeks their costliest week is more than twice the reference's.
Rules that estimate weights on the simplex, with a shared structure across areas, do not fail in this way.
With a scarcity-level critical ratio of 0.99 for every area, soft GDMA's total cost is 11\% below the reference forecaster's, as before (0.888), its CVaR$_{95}$ is 8\% lower and its costliest week 10\% lower; forecast-then-commit and the quantile-regression combination become more expensive than the reference in total (1.18--1.53) and 14--15 times as costly in their costliest week.
Equal weights, which give weight to naive rules that over-commit, have the lightest tail at this critical ratio (costliest week 0.66) at a higher total cost (1.071), a reminder that the ranking by total cost is not the ranking by tail risk.

\paragraph{New and data-poor systems}
Fifty-two areas enter the panel after the start of the evaluation: the 46 U.S.\ balancing authorities in January 2019, the five Thai regions in January 2023 and one European system.
During their first eight weeks each of them has only a few weeks of history (Table~\ref{tab:entry}).
No combination does well in this phase: all of them except the quantile-regression combination (0.976) are more expensive than the reference forecaster during the first eight weeks.
Pooled weights, which impose the rule learned from other systems, are 61\% more expensive, forecast-then-commit 39--40\% more expensive, and the neural gate, whose features are not yet informative, 2.6 times as expensive.
Plain GDMA (1.053) and fair soft GDMA (1.058) are the closest to the reference among the rules that share structure across areas, followed by soft GDMA and the best single candidate (1.069, each); per-area weights, which rely on the area's own few days, and shrinkage are 11\% more expensive.
Two-step clustering (1.170) does less well for entering areas than for the established ones, because it clusters weights that are themselves estimated from a handful of days.
The quantile-regression combination is better than the reference for entering areas but worse for established ones (1.016); we have no explanation for this reversal and the entry sample is small.
In the U.S.\ part of the sample, where an official forecast exists, an operator adopting any of these rules would do well to keep committing on it for the first weeks of a new unit.

\paragraph{Segments across continents}
GDMA uses more than one segment in 89\% of the weekly re-estimations (two segments in 20\%, three in 22\%, four in 21\%), much more often than in the U.S.\ panel, and soft GDMA shrinks towards the segment rule with an average intensity of $\kappa=0.39$.
Segments are not continents.
Among weeks with several segments, two systems on the same continent share a segment only 37--49\% of the time (North America 46\%, Europe 49\%, Oceania 42\%, Asia 37\%), European and African systems are grouped together 52\% of the time, and North American areas are grouped with European or Asian ones only 19--21\% of the time.
The segmentation therefore does not follow geography; it is consistent with differences in which forecasting systems work---notably whether an operator's own forecast is available and informative---which a planner could not have specified in advance.

Table~\ref{tab:fairness} asks how the gains are distributed, relative to the reference forecaster and relative to each area's own best single candidate.
Against the reference forecaster the picture is mixed: even the best rules make 17--21\% of the areas worse off, because the reference is often a strong forecast (the operator's own, or a model forecast that is also one of the candidates), and rules that impose a common rule or learn a flexible global map do worse: the neural gate makes 34\% of the areas worse off, forecast-then-commit 41--45\% and the quantile-regression combination 58\%.
Against each area's own best single candidate the groups separate more clearly: soft GDMA makes only 6\% of the areas worse off and its fair variant 3\%, whereas pooled weights make 24\% worse off, per-area weights and two-step clustering 14\%, the neural gate 42\%, forecast-then-commit 52--60\% and the quantile-regression combination 86\%.
The median area in the worst-served continent is 2.2--2.8\% better off than under the reference under the GDMA variants and the clustering rules, and worse off under the neural gate (1.010), forecast-then-commit (1.047--1.053) and the quantile-regression combination (1.127).
A large gap remains between data-rich and data-poor systems: areas with at least two years of data save 12\% under soft GDMA, areas with less than two years (the Chinese provinces and Algeria) only 4\%, and per-area weights save almost nothing for the latter (0.983); only pooled weights, which impose the rule of other systems, do nearly as well for data-poor as for data-rich areas (0.922 against 0.901), at a higher cost for the rich ones.

Fair soft GDMA, which counts every area's relative improvement equally, is also the cheapest method in total (0.881 against 0.884, significant at the 1\% level), although within the model confidence set and economically negligible.
So in this panel fairness in the sense of the criterion costs essentially nothing in total cost, and shifts the distribution: the share of areas worse off than their own best candidate halves (6\% to 3\%), the 10th percentile of the ratio falls to 0.840 and its 90th to 1.022.
Weighting by relative cost prevents the few largest systems from dictating the segment rules, which is consistent with the segment-structure argument of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, under which fair and efficient criteria share their minimiser; we cannot tell from these data whether segments are exact.

\paragraph{Income}
Fairness at this scale is also a question of income.
We attach to every grid area the GDP per capita at purchasing-power parity of its country and its World Bank income group \citep{WorldBankWDI}, with Taiwan from the IMF World Economic Outlook \citep{IMFWEO}; 83 areas are in high-income and 42 in upper-middle-income countries.
For the U.S.\ balancing authorities we also compute the per-capita personal income of the states they serve \citep{BEApcpi}, weighted by approximate service-territory shares.

Across the world, areas in upper-middle-income countries save less than areas in high-income countries under every decision-focused simplex method (soft GDMA 4.9\% against 12.2\%), but this gap largely coincides with the data gap above, because the upper-middle-income group is dominated by the Chinese provinces with a single year of data.
Across individual areas, the rank correlation between an area's cost ratio and its GDP per capita is $-0.12$ for soft GDMA (90\% bootstrap interval $-0.26$ to $0.03$) and $-0.11$ for its fair variant, so richer areas gain slightly more but the interval includes zero; it is positive and significant for pooled weights ($0.19$, interval $0.04$ to $0.34$), which favour poorer areas, and not different from zero for the neural gate.
Among the 88 areas with at least two years of data it is positive for each of the methods we checked ($0.17$ for soft GDMA, $0.18$ for its fair variant, $0.23$ for pooled weights and $0.39$ for the neural gate), that is, richer areas gain \emph{less}.
The sign is therefore not stable across subsamples, as one would expect if income itself plays no role and the correlation reflects data length and continent, with which income is confounded.
Within the United States, in cost-weighted terms the balancing authorities in the highest third of state income gain more (cost ratios of 0.68 under soft GDMA against 0.93 in the lowest third), but this reflects a few large, high-income systems whose own official forecasts happen to be weak; across balancing authorities the rank correlation with state income is $0.03$ (90\% interval $-0.24$ to $0.31$).
We therefore find no evidence that GDMA systematically favours richer or poorer systems, but with 125 areas, income measured at the level of countries and states, and strong confounding with data length, the data cannot rule out moderate gradients either.
